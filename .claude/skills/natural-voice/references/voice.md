# Voice profile: default

These rules cover anything you write for another person to read: texts, emails, letters, notes, posts and cards. Apply them to every draft before you send it. Only text labeled "Bad:" in this file breaks them.

The word lists behind several rules live in `word-list.md`, next to this file. "Word list: X" below names a section of it.

## Contents

- How to write: shape, register, dates, secondhand news, caveats, voice

## How to write

### Shape

Lead with the point. The first sentence carries the news or the ask, and the details follow.

- Bad: "So I've been thinking about it a lot, and after talking it over with Sam and looking at the calendar, we've decided we won't make it to the reunion."
- Better: "We can't make the reunion. Sam's sister is getting married that weekend."

Break text into short paragraphs with a blank line between them. Keep each paragraph to one idea in four sentences or fewer. When someone asks for "natural line breaks" or "better readability," they want the wall of text split into paragraphs. Change the wording only if they ask for that too.

### Register

Use plain words and concrete specifics. A name or a number tells the reader more than an adjective does.

### Dates

In a message the reader sees soon after you send it, say when things happened relative to now: "yesterday," "on Monday," "last week." Switch to the exact date once it's more than a couple of weeks back. Writing meant to be read long after, such as a letter someone will keep or a journal entry, keeps exact dates.

- Bad (sent two days later): "I ran into Maria on October 26."
- Better: "I ran into Maria on Monday."

### Secondhand news

When you pass along something a person told you privately, say who told you and when, and quote the line that settles it.

- Bad: "Apparently the party got moved."
- Better: "Jess texted me last night: 'Party's moved to Sunday, same time.'"

### Caveats

Do not tack an unrequested caveat, counterpoint, disclaimer or moral onto a clear claim to show balance. If a condition changes whether the claim holds, work it into the claim. Cut any caveat the claim survives unchanged. Most balancing caveats protect the writer, so before keeping one, ask what the reader could do with it.

- Bad: "The bakery on Elm makes the best croissants in town. Of course, taste is subjective, and everyone's experience is different."
- Better: "The bakery on Elm makes the best croissants in town. Go before nine, because they sell out."

### Voice

Have opinions. When someone asks what you think, give your answer first and then your reason. If one thing you don't know could change your answer, name it once.

Use "I" when you're speaking for yourself. "I loved the second half of the book" tells your friend whose opinion it is.

## Constructions to avoid

Each entry has a definition and a Bad/Better pair.

### Contrast and negation

#### Antithesis

A sentence that asserts X by rejecting Y, usually "X, not Y."

- Bad: "It's a home, not a house."
- Better: "We've lived here eleven years, and both kids learned to walk in this kitchen."

#### Corrective negation

Denying a claim nobody made so the next sentence can correct it.

- Bad: "This isn't goodbye. It's see you later."
- Better: "I move on Saturday, and I'll be back for Thanksgiving."

#### Negative parallelisms

"Not only X but Y" and "It's not just X, it's Y" frames that inflate a plain statement.

- Bad: "It's not just a dinner, it's a celebration of everything we've been through."
- Better: "Dinner is at seven to celebrate Dana's new job."

#### Negative anaphora

The same negation repeated at the start of successive clauses or sentences.

- Bad: "No phones at the table. No TV during dinner. No excuses."
- Better: "Phones go in the basket by the door until dinner's over."

#### Contrasting pairs

Two opposites set in matching form for the sound of it, such as "less stuff, more memories" or "small town, big heart." A real tradeoff stated plainly is fine: "The cabin is quiet and an hour from the nearest store."

- Bad: "Less planning, more living."
- Better: "We didn't book anything past the first night, and that's how we found the hot springs."

### Paragraph structure

#### Paragraph pinning

A one-sentence paragraph used to hammer home the point of the paragraph before it.

- Bad: "Grandpa kept every letter Grandma sent him during the war, tied with a ribbon in his sock drawer. [new paragraph] That's love."
- Better: "Grandpa kept every letter Grandma sent him during the war, tied with a ribbon in his sock drawer."

#### Parataxis

A run of short clauses with no connectors, used for drama. Join related clauses with because, so, while or after. A plain fact followed by its reason can stand as two sentences. The rule targets runs cut short for effect.

- Bad: "The power went out. We lit candles. Nobody complained."
- Better: "When the power went out we lit candles, and the kids thought it was the best night of the trip."

#### Summary beats

A sentence that restates what the paragraph already said, often opening with "In short," "Bottom line" or "The takeaway."

- Bad: "...and it rained every day we were there. In short, the weather ruined the trip."
- Better: "...and it rained every day we were there, so we spent most of the week playing cards in the lodge."

#### Landing sentences

A closing line written to sound wise or quotable after the paragraph has made its point. Generic upbeat endings belong here too, such as "exciting times ahead" or "here's to the next chapter."

- Bad: "And sometimes, that's all you need."
- Better: "We're going back in June."

#### Setup/payoff

Holding back the point so it can be revealed next, usually through a question or a colon. A colon can introduce a list or an expected explanation, as long as the main point already sits before it.

- Bad: "The secret to her lasagna? Cottage cheese."
- Better: "Her lasagna uses cottage cheese in place of ricotta."

#### Rhetorical crutches

Stock phrases that announce a point without making it. Word list: Stock openers and crutches.

- Bad: "Here's the thing: the dog needs more exercise."
- Better: "The dog chewed through the couch cushion again, so I'm adding a second walk."

### Rhythm

#### Rule of three

Grouping items or adjectives in threes for cadence. In prose, cut a run of three down to the ones that matter, or give one its own sentence. Bulleted lists hold however many items there are.

- Bad: "The wedding was beautiful, heartfelt and unforgettable."
- Better: "Half the guests were crying before the vows."

#### Parallel sentence structures within a paragraph

Consecutive sentences built on the same grammatical frame. This applies to running prose. List items may share a frame.

- Bad: "My mom taught me to cook. My dad taught me to fish."
- Better: "My mom taught me to cook, and I learned to fish from my dad on Saturday mornings at the reservoir."

#### Uniform sentence length

Sentences of the same length in a row read as machine output. Vary length unpredictably.

- Bad: "We got to the beach at noon. The water was too cold to swim. We ate lunch on the sand."
- Better: "We got to the beach at noon. The water was so cold that only my nephew went in, and he lasted about a minute before running back for a towel."

### Punctuation and openers

#### Em dashes

Use at most one em dash in a paragraph, and prefer none. En dashes used as punctuation count as em dashes. Replace the rest according to the grammar:

- Two independent clauses: a full stop. This is the most common fix.
- A list or appositive in the middle of a sentence: commas, or a real list.
- An explanation or definition: a colon.
- An aside: parentheses or commas.
- An afterthought at the end: a full stop, or delete it if it adds nothing.

Use a hyphen for ranges (10-20).

- Bad: "The hike was short—two miles—but the view was worth it."
- Better: "The hike is two miles, and from the top you can see the whole lake."

#### Throat-clearing openers

Words before the content that announce what's coming.

- Bad: "So, I just wanted to reach out and let you know that I won't be at book club."
- Better: "I won't be at book club Thursday. My flight lands at eight."

### Word choice

#### Stacked noun phrases

Three or more nouns chained as modifiers, which the reader has to unpack backwards.

- Bad: "the family reunion dinner seating chart problem"
- Better: "the trouble we're having deciding who sits where at the reunion dinner"

#### Nominalization

A verb turned into a noun, which then needs a weak verb to carry it.

- Bad: "We made the decision to have a celebration of her birthday at the lake."
- Better: "We're celebrating her birthday at the lake."

#### Hedging qualifiers

Words that soften a claim without adding information. Word list: Hedging qualifiers. When the uncertainty is real, say it once and say where it comes from.

- Bad: "It might potentially rain a bit at some point tomorrow."
- Better: "The forecast says rain after three, so bring a jacket."

#### Filler intensifiers and filler phrases

Cut intensifiers. The sentence means the same without them. Filler phrases pad a short idea, so replace them. Word list: Filler intensifiers and filler phrases.

- Bad: "I just really wanted to say that I truly appreciated the flowers, due to the fact that it was a hard week."
- Better: "Thank you for the flowers. They came on the worst day of a hard week."

#### Corporate-register verbs

Verbs from slide decks and press releases. Word list: Corporate-register verbs. Literal uses such as "drive to the coast" or "the lake reflects the mountains" are fine.

- Bad: "We're leveraging the long weekend to facilitate some quality family time."
- Better: "We're taking the kids camping over the long weekend."

### Tone

#### Formal register

Formal written register hides who's doing what. Write it the way you'd say it out loud to a friend.

- Bad: "Your attendance at the gathering would be greatly appreciated."
- Better: "We'd love for you to come."

#### Performed enthusiasm

Excitement put on for effect, through exclamation points or stock phrases like "thrilled to share."

- Bad: "Thrilled to announce that we got a puppy!!! Our hearts are so full!"
- Better: "We got a puppy. She's a beagle mix named Pickle, and she has already eaten a shoe."

## Other signs of AI writing

Negative parallelisms, rule of three, em dashes, filler, hedging and generic upbeat endings live in the entries above.

### Content

#### Inflated significance

Claims that an ordinary event marks a turning point or stands for something larger. Word list: Inflated significance.

- Bad: "Our move to Denver marks a pivotal moment in our family's journey."
- Better: "We're moving to Denver in May to be closer to my parents."

#### Notability claims

Listing press coverage or follower counts to prove something matters.

- Bad: "The restaurant has been featured in Food & Wine, Eater and the Times, and has 200,000 Instagram followers."
- Better: "The chef makes the tortillas by hand, and the line is out the door on Fridays."

#### Superficial -ing analyses

Participle phrases tacked onto a sentence to add fake depth. Word list: Superficial -ing analyses.

- Bad: "She planted a lemon tree in the yard, symbolizing new beginnings and reflecting her love of home."
- Better: "She planted a lemon tree in the yard the week they moved in."

#### Promotional language

Ad copy in place of plain description. Word list: Promotional language and hype.

- Bad: "Nestled in the hills, the town boasts breathtaking views and a vibrant food scene."
- Better: "The town sits in the hills above the river, and the taco truck by the bridge is worth the drive."

#### Vague attributions

Opinions credited to unnamed authorities: "experts say," "studies show," "people say," "some argue."

- Bad: "Experts say dogs are happier with a routine."
- Better: "Our vet said Pickle would settle down faster if she ate and walked at the same times every day."

#### Formulaic challenges sections

A "Despite these challenges" or "looking ahead" passage that names problems vaguely and ends upbeat.

- Bad: "Despite the challenges of the year, our family continues to grow stronger."
- Better: "Dad's knee surgery took most of the spring, and he's back to walking the dog every morning."

### Language

#### AI vocabulary

Words that show up far more in model output than in human writing and tend to cluster. Word list: AI vocabulary.

- Bad: "Additionally, the trip let us delve into the rich tapestry of local culture."
- Better: "We also spent a morning at the fish market, where a vendor showed my daughter how to shuck an oyster."

#### Copula avoidance

"Serves as" or "features" where "is" or "has" would do. Word list: Copula avoidance.

- Bad: "The guest room serves as an office and features a pull-out couch."
- Better: "The guest room is also my office. It has a pull-out couch."

#### Synonym cycling

Renaming the same thing in each sentence to avoid repetition, which makes the reader wonder whether these are different things.

- Bad: "My grandmother raised six kids. The matriarch also ran the farm. The family elder still bakes every Sunday."
- Better: "My grandmother raised six kids and ran the farm, and she still bakes every Sunday."

#### False ranges

"From X to Y" where X and Y sit on no real scale.

- Bad: "The book covers everything from first love to the meaning of life."
- Better: "The book follows two sisters through the summer before one of them leaves for college."

### Formatting

#### Boldface overuse

Bold scattered across phrases for emphasis the sentence should carry on its own.

- Bad: "Please bring **a dish to share** and **your own chairs**."
- Better: "Please bring a dish to share and something to sit on."

#### Inline-header lists

Bullets that open with a bolded label and a colon, often repeating the label in the sentence after it.

- Bad: "- **Food:** Food will be provided by the hosts."
- Better: "We'll cook, so you only need to bring yourselves."

#### Title case in headings

Capitalizing every main word of a heading. Use sentence case.

- Bad: "Our Summer Trip To The Coast"
- Better: "Our summer trip to the coast"

#### Emojis

Decorating headings or bullets with emoji. Leave them out.

- Bad: a balloon emoji before "Party details: Saturday at 2"
- Better: "The party is Saturday at 2."

#### Curly quotes

Typographic quotation marks in place of straight ones, a common sign of pasted model output.

- Bad: She said “see you soon” and hung up.
- Better: She said "see you soon" and hung up.

### Document level

Read the whole document before judging single sentences. These only show across a page.

#### Repeated stock phrases

The same opener or hedge in paragraph after paragraph: "we would like," "To be clear," "One more on." Each sentence reads fine alone. Three or more of one phrase is a finding. After rewriting, count again, because the replacement can become the new repeat.

- Bad: "We would like to confirm the dates. We would like to check the price. We would also like to know who is coming."
- Better: "Can you confirm the dates and the price? It would also help to know who is coming."

#### Signposting transitions

A sentence that announces the next point instead of making it: "There is a second half to this," "One more on the same subject," "Worth being clear about."

- Bad: "There is a second half to this, which is the payment date."
- Better: "The second question is how far the first payment date can move."

#### Abstract nouns as metaphor

An abstract noun standing in for a literal one: "the shape of the request," "every shape we tried." Use the literal word: format, structure, field, version.

- Bad: "We tried every shape of the booking form."
- Better: "We tried three versions of the booking form."

#### Facts that do not add up

Counts that do not sum, or a claim contradicted further down. This is not a sign of AI writing, but report it. Never invent the missing fact: drop the unsupported figure or ask the writer.

### Communication

#### Chatbot artifacts and sycophancy

Correspondence pasted in as content, or praise for the question or the person before the answer: "Of course!", "Great question!", "Here is a summary," "I hope this helps," "Let me know if you'd like me to expand."

- Bad: "Great question! Here's what I'd suggest for the toast. I hope this helps!"
- Better: "Start the toast with the story about the canoe."

#### Firm or defensive register

In letters and questions to a supplier, partner or anyone outside, phrasing that pre-empts objections and sounds curt: "we need confirmed," "we do not want to find," "is not a problem for us. We are not asking for one," a bold **not**. Write plain requests and neutral statements of what follows. Keep the question itself clear.

- Bad: "We need confirmed which date you intend. We do not want to find out in November."
- Better: "Please tell us which date you intend. It would help to know now rather than in November."

#### Knowledge-cutoff disclaimers

Hedges about what the writer could find out, left in the text.

- Bad: "While details about the event are limited, it appears to start sometime in the evening."
- Better: "The concert starts at 7:30. Doors open at 6:45."

## Pre-send checklist

Run this over every draft before you send it.

- Check that the first sentence carries the point and that the opening holds no throat-clearing, praise or chatbot opener.
- Confirm no paragraph runs past four sentences or covers two ideas, and that a blank line follows each one.
- Merge any one-sentence paragraph that only repeats the point before it, and rewrite any question or colon that holds back the point.
- Search for em dashes (—) and en dashes (–). Keep at most one per paragraph, rewrite the rest by grammar, and use a hyphen for ranges.
- Read each "not," "isn't," "but" and "yet" and rewrite any that set up antithesis, corrective negation, a negative parallelism or a contrasting pair.
- Delete the last sentence of a paragraph if removing it loses nothing the reader needs.
- Find every caveat, disclaimer, counterpoint, offer to say more and upbeat ending. Fold a caveat into the claim if it changes the claim, and delete the rest.
- Find every run of three items or adjectives in prose and cut it to the ones that matter.
- Break up neighboring sentences that share a frame, and any three in a row of about the same length.
- Scan for every word and phrase in `word-list.md`.
- Replace each vague claim with a name or a number, or cut it.
- In a message read soon after sending, turn recent exact dates into relative ones, and name the source of any secondhand news.
- Rewrite any sentence that hides who did what.
- Check headings for sentence case, strip decorative bold and emoji, and straighten curly quotes.
- Count repeated openers and hedges across the whole text, rewrite past the second use, and count again after the fixes.
- In a message to someone outside, rewrite firm or defensive phrasing as a plain request.

## Do not over-correct

- One group of three, used on purpose, can stay.
- A strong, specific verb is not a tell because it is vivid.
- Real enthusiasm from a writer or brand that is enthusiastic can stay.
- Text that is specific, varied and earns its claims stays, even when it is polished. Polish is not the tell; vagueness, symmetry and hype are.
